# Lesson 1.3: Building scoring rubrics for practical tasks

A practical task is only fair if two trainers would score the same work the same way. A rubric makes that possible:
it names what is checked (criteria) and describes, for each criterion, what the work looks like at each level. Every
practical task in Modules 2 to 5 uses the rubric format of this lesson.

> **Note**
> The rubrics in this course are the trainer's own material, not an official certification. Change the criteria and
> points to fit your course.

## Learning objectives

After this lesson, learners can:

- turn the steps of a practical task into three to six criteria;
- describe four levels per criterion by evidence that can be seen on the installation;
- give points per level and write a pass rule;
- compare their scores with another trainer's and settle differences.

## Trainer's notes

### How to present it

1. **The format** (4 minutes). Every rubric in this course looks like this:

   | Criterion | 0: Not shown | 1: Partly | 2: Mostly | 3: Fully | Evidence |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | What is checked | nothing done, or wrong | started, important parts missing | done with a minor fault | done as asked | where the trainer looks |

   The **Evidence** column says where to look: a page in the administration, a file, the output of a command.
2. **Describe what you see** (4 minutes). "Good understanding" cannot be checked. "The article is published below
   the learner's folder, at Version 2, with Intro filled in" can.
3. **Points and the pass rule** (3 minutes). The levels give 0 to 3 points. A pass rule in this course reads "at
   least two thirds of the points and no criterion at 0". Safety criteria (for example "no mail was sent to a real
   address") can be marked **must**: a 0 there means not passed, whatever the total.
4. **Calibration** (4 minutes). Before scoring a group, two trainers score the same two pieces of work alone and
   compare. Where they differ by more than one level, the level description is unclear; rewrite it.

### Common questions

**Why four levels, not ten?** Four levels can be told apart by looking. Ten cannot, and two trainers will disagree.

**May a learner see the rubric before the task?** Yes. Hand it out with the task: it tells learners what matters,
and it is no secret what a published article looks like.

**What if a learner solves the task another way?** If the result meets the criterion, it scores. A rubric checks the
result and the safety rules, not one click path.

### Pitfalls

- **Criteria that overlap.** "Article published" and "article visible on the site" score one fact twice.
- **A criterion for speed.** Time is set by the rules, not scored, unless the objective is speed.
- **Scoring from memory.** Write the evidence down while you look, or take a screenshot with the learner's
  permission.

## Hands-on exercise

Build a rubric for this task: "In your course folder, create an article with Title, Intro and Body, publish it, edit
the title once more and publish again, then hide the article."

1. List the steps of the task.
2. Turn them into three or four criteria, for example *Article created in the right place*, *Required fields filled*,
   *Second version published*, *Item hidden*.
3. For each criterion write the four level descriptions as things you can see.
4. Write the **Evidence** column: for example the article's page, its **Manage versions** page and the tree, which
   marks hidden items ([Publishing, versions and the trash](../../../publishing-and-versions.md#hide-an-item)).
5. Write the points and a pass rule.
6. With a neighbor, score the same imagined piece of work: "Article published at Version 1 only, hidden, Intro
   filled." Compare the scores.
7. Where you differ by more than one level, rewrite the description that caused it.

## Expected result

- A rubric of three or four criteria with four levels each and an evidence column.
- Two trainers scoring the same work differ by at most one level on any criterion.
- The trainer shows one rubric on the projector and asks the group to score the example in step 6.

## Quiz

1. What does the **Evidence** column of a rubric say?
2. Which level description can be checked? A. "Understands versions" B. "Article shows Version 2 on its page" C.
   "Works carefully" D. "Good effort"
3. What does a **must** criterion mean for the pass rule?
4. What do you do when two trainers score the same work two levels apart?
5. A learner reached the result by another click path than the one you taught. How do you score it?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Where the trainer looks to decide the level: a page, a file or a command's output.
2. **B.** It can be seen on the item's page
   ([Publishing, versions and the trash](../../../publishing-and-versions.md#drafts-and-versions)).
3. A 0 on it means the task is not passed, whatever the total.
4. Rewrite the level description that caused the difference, then score again.
5. As the result deserves: the rubric checks the result and the safety rules, not the path.

</details>
